Foundation

1. What Is a Cottage Cluster?

A cottage cluster is a group of relatively small, detached homes oriented around shared open space, such as a courtyard, garden, walkway, or common green.

The homes are designed as a connected neighborhood rather than as unrelated houses that happen to sit near each other.

The design starts with people—not parking. Parking, access, utilities, privacy, and maintenance still matter, but they support the shared living pattern instead of becoming the main visual feature.
Development Lens

2. Why Developers Care

Developers see more than vacant land. They evaluate infrastructure, utilities, grading, stormwater, density, approvals, construction efficiency, ownership structure, and long-term operating responsibility.

A successful cottage cluster must work as both a place to live and a complete development system.

Local Atlas

3. Jackson County & Southern Oregon

Southern Oregon faces housing shortages, wildfire rebuilding pressure, rising land costs, and demand for smaller homes. Cottage clusters may offer another housing form, but whether a project works depends on the exact jurisdiction, zoning, utilities, access, wildfire standards, stormwater requirements, and development rules.

Never assume that a project seen in another city can be copied directly onto a Southern Oregon property. The concept may transfer; the approvals and site design may not.

Design Precedents

4. Well-Known Cottage Clusters

This section is intentionally text-based. The goal is to study the neighborhood pattern—not to frame the lesson around one house or a photo gallery.

Third Street Cottages

Langley, Washington

Eight compact cottages were organized around a shared garden. The project became one of the best-known modern examples of a pocket neighborhood and demonstrated how several smaller homes could create less visual impact than fewer oversized houses.

Lesson: zoning innovation and shared-space design can work together.

Greenwood Avenue Cottages

Shoreline, Washington

Eight small homes face a common green, while garages and parking are placed to the side. Private yards and layered outdoor spaces help balance community interaction with personal privacy.

Lesson: shared space works best when privacy is deliberately designed too.

Salish Pond Cottages

Fairview, Oregon

This Oregon pocket-neighborhood precedent is often discussed as an example of compact homes grouped around common space. It shows how cottage-style development can adapt to Pacific Northwest communities outside a major city center.

Lesson: the model can be adapted to Oregon conditions, but local code still controls.

Conover Commons

Redmond, Washington

This larger pocket-neighborhood precedent uses clusters of homes, pedestrian-oriented common areas, and a layered transition between shared and private space.

Lesson: cottage-neighborhood principles can scale beyond one tiny cluster.

Important distinction: These are design precedents. A well-known pocket neighborhood is not automatically identical to the legal definition of a cottage cluster in Oregon or in a specific city or county.

Reference starting points: Ross Chapin Architects’ project records for Third Street Cottages, Greenwood Avenue Cottages, Salish Pond Cottages, and Conover Commons. Current local zoning and development rules must always be checked separately.

Builder Lens

5. Thinking Like a Builder

  • How will equipment, deliveries, emergency vehicles, and workers access the site?
  • Where will water, sewer, power, communications, and stormwater systems run?
  • Can foundations, framing, roofs, and finishes be standardized efficiently?
  • How will grading and drainage protect every cottage and the shared space?
  • Who maintains the common walkways, landscaping, lighting, and utilities?
Developer Lens

6. Thinking Like a Developer

  • How many homes are permitted?
  • Is the site large enough after setbacks, access, parking, utilities, and common space?
  • What approvals, studies, professionals, and financing are required?
  • Will the homes be rentals, individually owned, condominium units, or held under another structure?
  • Who is the intended resident or buyer?
  • What is the backup plan if the preferred design is not approved?
Property Screening

7. Questions to Ask About Any Property

  • What is the exact zoning and jurisdiction?
  • Is public water available?
  • Is public sewer available, or would septic be required?
  • Are there easements, access limitations, or private-road obligations?
  • What setbacks, lot coverage, height, parking, and common-space standards apply?
  • Are cottage clusters or another form of middle housing permitted?
  • Are wildfire, flood, slope, wetland, or environmental constraints present?
  • Can the site support the full utility and stormwater plan?
