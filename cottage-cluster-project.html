The complete development path

From idea to operating community.

1

Start with the housing idea

Define who the homes are for, whether they will be rentals or individually owned, expected unit sizes, accessibility goals, and why a cluster is a better fit than detached lots or a larger apartment building.

2

Find a possible parcel

Record the parcel number, owner, acreage, zoning, plan designation, access, topography, nearby utilities, flood or wetland indicators, and the surrounding neighborhood pattern.

3

Read the actual local rules

Confirm whether cottage clusters are allowed, the minimum and maximum unit count, cottage footprint limits, common-courtyard standards, setbacks, height, parking, access, landscaping, and approval type.

4

Sketch the site around people

Place the common courtyard first. Orient entrances and pedestrian paths toward it. Then solve privacy, parking, trash, mail, fire access, utilities, stormwater, storage, and service access.

5

Choose the ownership and operating model

Compare one-owner rental, condominium, individual lots through a middle-housing land division, cooperative ownership, affordable-housing ownership, or another qualified legal structure.

6

Build the real development budget

Include land, closing, survey, design, engineering, land use, building permits, utilities, road work, stormwater, fire requirements, financing, insurance, taxes, holding costs, construction, contingency, marketing, and operations.

7

Assemble the professional team

Identify the planner, surveyor, civil engineer, architect or designer, geotechnical or environmental professionals, lender, attorney, contractor, utility contacts, property manager, and insurance broker.

8

Verify before spending heavily

Use a pre-application meeting, written planning questions, title review, survey research, utility letters, preliminary engineering, and a feasibility period to test the concept.

9

Move through approvals and construction

Track applications, conditions of approval, final plans, permits, fees, inspections, change orders, utility coordination, public improvements, and closeout documents.

10

Operate or sell the completed community

Create maintenance, reserves, common-area rules, landscaping, insurance, management, leasing or resale, resident communication, and long-term governance systems.

Cottage Cluster Master Checklist

Project progress0 / 20

Planning

Is the use allowed? What is the approval type? Which cottage, courtyard, parking, height, setback, tree, accessibility, and design rules apply? Are current rule changes pending?

Utilities

Can water, sewer, stormwater, power, and fire service support the proposed units? Who pays for extensions, upgrades, meters, easements, and system charges?

Ownership

Will the project be one rental property, condominium, individual lots, cooperative housing, affordable housing, or another structure? How are common areas governed and funded?

Finance

What loan covers land, predevelopment, construction, and stabilization? What equity and reserves are required? What happens if approvals or construction take longer?

Land-use planner

Interprets zoning, approval path, standards, and local process.

Surveyor

Researches boundaries, plats, easements, and land-division work.

Civil engineer

Roads, grading, utilities, drainage, and public improvements.

Designer or architect

Cottages, courtyard, accessibility, privacy, and code coordination.

Attorney and CPA

Entity, ownership, condominium or lot structure, contracts, and taxes.

Contractor and manager

Constructability, pricing, phasing, operations, maintenance, and turnover.

Property file

Deed, title report, tax records, maps, surveys, easements, access, environmental, and utility information.

Approval file

Planning notes, applications, staff responses, conditions, drawings, engineering, fees, permits, and inspections.

Money file

Land contract, studies, development budget, financing, invoices, draws, taxes, insurance, contingency, and operating pro forma.

Operations file

Ownership documents, common-area rules, reserves, maintenance, landscaping, insurance, leases or sales, and resident communication.
