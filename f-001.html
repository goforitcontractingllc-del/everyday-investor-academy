Start With the Question in Front of You

Turn overwhelm into one clear question, three facts, and one next move.

ADHD-friendly Practical 15–25 minutes Printable
Real-life opening

Why this matters

You can know that money, housing, credit, work, land, and family all matter—and still have no clue where to start. The mistake is trying to solve the whole life at once. Foundation begins by shrinking the problem until your brain can actually hold it.

Quick answer

A useful starting question is specific enough to act on, small enough to answer, and connected to a real decision.

Deep dive

Open only what you need.

The full lesson is here, but you do not have to read it in one sitting.

Big goals are emotionally loud but operationally vague. “Fix my finances” is not a next step. “What bill becomes dangerous first if I do nothing this week?” is.

Write one sentence describing the decision. Then list what you know, what you are assuming, and what must be verified.

Feelings matter because they show pressure, fear, or desire. But the decision still needs facts. Keep both columns instead of pretending one cancels the other.

Choose the smallest action that creates better information: pull the report, call the office, measure the site, compare the bill, or write the scope.

Common mistakes

What usually goes wrong

Trying to solve everything

Too many open loops create paralysis. Reduce the lesson to one current decision.

Using assumptions as facts

Write assumptions down so they can be verified instead of silently controlling the decision.

Waiting for perfect certainty

Most real decisions require a reasonable next step, not complete knowledge.

Skipping the record

Notes, screenshots, calculations, and dates protect memory and improve future decisions.

Real-life challenge

Use this today

Choose one real decision you are avoiding. Reduce it to one question, three facts, one unknown, and one action you can complete within 24 hours.
