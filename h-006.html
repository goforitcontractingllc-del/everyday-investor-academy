How Mortgages Work

A mortgage is a long-term loan secured by real property. The payment is more than interest, and approval is not the same as affordability.

20–30 minute readBeginner friendlyEvergreen core

Start with the simple version

A mortgage allows a buyer to purchase a home using borrowed money. The home becomes collateral for the loan. If the borrower stops meeting the loan obligations, the lender may eventually use a legal foreclosure process to recover the property.

Core idea: A mortgage is not the house. It is the financing agreement attached to the purchase of the house.

The pieces of a mortgage payment

PrincipalThe portion that reduces the amount still owed on the loan.
InterestThe lender's charge for providing the money.
Property taxesTaxes charged by local government and often collected through escrow.
Homeowners insuranceCoverage required by most lenders to protect the property.
Mortgage insuranceCoverage that may be required when the buyer has a smaller down payment.
HOA or other feesUsually paid separately, but still part of the real monthly housing cost.

Why the early payments feel slow

Most standard mortgages are amortized. Early in the loan, a larger portion of each payment goes toward interest. Over time, more of the payment shifts toward principal.

Learning example

A buyer borrows $300,000. Their monthly principal-and-interest payment may stay fixed on a fixed-rate loan, but the amount applied to principal increases over time while the interest portion decreases.

Fixed-rate versus adjustable-rate

Fixed-rate mortgageThe interest rate stays the same for the agreed term. Taxes and insurance can still change.
Adjustable-rate mortgageThe rate may change after an initial period according to the loan terms and an external index.

Loan term

A longer term usually lowers the required monthly principal-and-interest payment but may increase total interest paid. A shorter term usually requires a higher payment but can reduce the total interest cost.

What escrow does

An escrow account is a holding account used by the loan servicer to collect money for certain future bills, commonly property taxes and homeowners insurance. The monthly payment can change if those bills change.

Preapproval is not a promise

A preapproval is an early lender estimate based on the information available at that time. Final approval still depends on updated income, debt, credit, property condition, appraisal, title, insurance, and other underwriting requirements.

What happens when payments are missed

A missed payment may create late fees, credit damage, collection activity, and eventually foreclosure risk. Borrowers should contact the servicer early rather than waiting until the problem becomes larger.

Refinancing

Refinancing replaces the existing mortgage with a new loan. It may lower the rate, change the term, remove or add borrowers, or access equity. It also creates new closing costs and can restart the amortization timeline.

Before choosing a mortgage

  • Compare rate, annual percentage rate, fees, and total cash needed.
  • Understand whether the rate is fixed or adjustable.
  • Know the full monthly payment, not only principal and interest.
  • Keep emergency reserves after closing.
  • Ask what could cause the payment to change.
  • Review prepayment and refinance assumptions carefully.
