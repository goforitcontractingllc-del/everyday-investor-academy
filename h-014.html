Mobile Home Parks

Understand what it means to own a manufactured home while renting the land beneath it—and why the park agreement matters as much as the home itself.

20–30 minutes Beginner friendly Housing systems Living Atlas connected
Why this matters: In many mobile home parks, the resident owns the home but rents the space. That creates a hybrid situation: part homeownership, part tenancy, and part community-rule system.
Important: Park rules, rent increases, sale requirements, relocation rights, notices, and resident protections can change by state and local law. Use the Living Atlas and official sources before acting.
📖 The Lesson

Two separate things may be involved

A resident may own the manufactured home itself while leasing the land, utility connections, parking, and shared facilities from the park owner.

The home

The structure may be owned, financed, insured, maintained, improved, sold, or inherited by the resident.

The space

The land is typically controlled through a rental agreement, park rules, and applicable landlord-tenant law.

The park agreement matters

Before buying a home inside a park, review the space rent, utilities, fees, rules, age restrictions if any, guest policies, parking, pets, exterior standards, maintenance duties, sale approval procedures, and notice requirements.

Buying the home is not the same as buying the land

A low purchase price can still come with meaningful monthly costs. Space rent, utilities, insurance, taxes, repairs, financing, and park fees all affect affordability.

Selling can involve two approvals

The resident may need to find a buyer for the home while the buyer separately qualifies to rent the space. A sale can therefore depend on financing, condition, title, park approval, and current rules.

Relocation is possible—but often difficult

Moving a manufactured home can require permits, transportation, utility disconnection, setup, foundation work, a suitable destination, and a home that can safely be transported. Older homes may be especially difficult or expensive to move.

Plain-English rule: Never evaluate only the home. Evaluate the home, the park, the agreement, the monthly costs, the resale path, and the relocation risk together.
🧠 Key Terms

Space Rent

The recurring payment for the land or lot where the home is located.

Park Rules

Written community standards governing use, appearance, conduct, parking, pets, guests, and other matters.

Manufactured Home Title

The ownership record for the home, which may be handled differently from land title.

Relocation Cost

The full cost of disconnecting, moving, setting up, permitting, and reconnecting the home elsewhere.

🏠 Perspective Lenses

Resident

Monthly affordability, rule stability, repairs, resale, and long-term security.

Buyer

Home condition, title, financing, park approval, and full monthly costs.

Park Owner

Infrastructure, rules, maintenance, compliance, collections, and resident communication.

Investor

Whether the investment is the home, the park, or both—and which risks belong to each.

⚠️ Common Mistakes
  • Looking only at the home's sale price.
  • Failing to read the park rules before buying.
  • Assuming the buyer can automatically remain in the park.
  • Ignoring future space-rent changes.
  • Assuming the home can easily be moved.
  • Skipping title, inspection, utility, and financing checks.
📝 Try It Yourself
  • Write the purchase price and every monthly cost separately.
  • Request the full rental agreement and park rules.
  • Check how a future buyer must qualify.
  • Identify who maintains the home, lot, utilities, roads, and shared areas.
  • Ask what would happen if the park closed or the home had to move.
  • Verify the home's title and legal status.
✅ Knowledge Check

1. In many parks, what does the resident own?

2. Is the purchase price the full housing cost?

3. Can park approval matter when the home is sold?

⭐ Field Note

A mobile home inside a park should be analyzed as a package: structure, space lease, rules, infrastructure, monthly costs, resale restrictions, and relocation risk.
