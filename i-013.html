Flipping Houses: When It Works—and When It Does Not

A flip is a short-hold investment that depends on buying well, controlling construction, managing time, and selling into a real market.

18–30 minutesDetailed lessonAtlas terms included
Why this matters: Flipping can produce a profit, but small errors in purchase price, scope, holding time, or resale value can erase the entire margin.
📖 The Lesson

The flip equation

A flip starts with a realistic after-repair value, then subtracts acquisition, renovation, financing, holding, selling, tax, and contingency costs.

What makes a flip workable

Discounted acquisition

The purchase price leaves enough room for all costs and a profit margin.

Controlled scope

The work is defined, permitted where required, and estimated with real labor and material costs.

Reliable timeline

Financing, utilities, inspections, subcontractors, and sale preparation are coordinated.

Evidence-based exit

Comparable sales support the finished value instead of wishful thinking.

Holding cost changes everything

Holding cost continues while work is delayed. Interest, taxes, insurance, utilities, security, landscaping, and opportunity cost all keep moving.

Flip rule: Profit is not the spread between purchase price and sale price. It is what remains after every cost and delay.

⚠️ Common Mistakes
  • Using ARV as a guaranteed sale price.
  • Ignoring selling commissions and closing costs.
  • Starting demolition before permits and scope are clear.
  • Underestimating carrying time.
📝 Try It Yourself
  • Build a full cost list for a sample flip.
  • Add a contingency and three months of delay.
  • Find three realistic comparable sales.
  • Write a backup hold or rental plan.
