Understanding Land

Land is more than an empty piece of ground. Every parcel carries location, legal identity, physical conditions, access, utilities, restrictions, market demand, and possible uses.

1.1 — What Land Actually Is

Land includes the surface, the legal parcel, and certain rights connected to ownership.

The value of land depends on what can legally and physically be done with it—not just how large it is.

A parcel can be valuable for housing, farming, business, conservation, storage, infrastructure, or future development.

1.2 — Major Land Categories

Residential land supports housing uses, subject to zoning and development rules.

Agricultural and forest land may carry special use limits, tax treatment, or resource protections.

Commercial, industrial, mixed-use, and public-purpose land follow different operating and infrastructure needs.

1.3 — Reading a Parcel

A parcel number or tax-lot number helps identify land in assessor and mapping systems.

A parcel map shows location and shape, but it is not automatically a legal boundary survey.

Tax records, zoning maps, title records, and surveys answer different questions.

1.4 — How Land Creates Value

Value can come from legal use, location, access, utilities, density, subdivision potential, income, scarcity, or future demand.

Entitlements, infrastructure, and approvals can increase value—but they also cost time and money.

Raw land, entitled land, improved lots, and finished development sites are different stages.

🧩 The Terms You Must Recognize

Parcel

A legally recognized piece of land.

Tax lot

An assessor mapping and taxation reference.

Acreage

The measured land area, which may differ from casual estimates.

Highest and best use

The most productive legally and physically possible use.

Entitlement

A government approval allowing a proposed use or development.

Improved land

Land with some combination of access, utilities, grading, or approvals.

🗺️ Real Parcel Scenario

You find five acres advertised as “ready to build.” The listing shows a tax-lot map but no survey, septic approval, road agreement, or utility letters.

Open the developer walkthrough
  1. Do not treat marketing language as proof.
  2. Identify the parcel through official assessor and GIS records.
  3. Check zoning, legal access, title, utilities, soils, slope, and environmental constraints.
  4. Ask what approvals have actually been issued and whether they remain valid.
  5. Price the parcel based on verified facts, not hoped-for use.

✅ Think Like a Developer

Your field assignment
