Utilities and Infrastructure

A parcel is not truly development-ready until water, wastewater, power, roads, drainage, communications, and emergency access are understood and financially achievable.

45–70 minutesMultiple micro-lessonsDeveloper thinkingScenario basedAtlas connected
Educational notice: This lesson teaches research and decision-making. It is not a survey, title opinion, engineering report, environmental determination, planning approval, or legal advice.
Why this matters: Infrastructure can cost more than the land itself and can completely change whether a project is feasible.
Micro-lessonsField scenarioDeveloper assignmentKnowledge check

4.1 — Water

Water may come from a public utility, private system, well, spring, or another approved source.

Availability is different from capacity: a line may be nearby but unable to serve the proposed use.

Connection fees, meter costs, extensions, pressure, fire flow, and water rights may matter.

4.2 — Sewer and Septic

Public sewer requires availability, capacity, connection approval, and often system-development charges.

On-site septic depends on soils, site evaluation, reserve area, setbacks, and health-department approval.

A failed septic assumption can make a parcel unusable for the intended density.

4.3 — Electricity, Gas, and Communications

Distance to service, transformer capacity, undergrounding, easements, and trenching can create major costs.

Internet and cellular coverage affect residents, businesses, and remote operations.

Utility-provider estimates should be obtained in writing when possible.

4.4 — Roads, Drainage, and Fire Access

Development may require road improvements, turnarounds, sidewalks, drainage facilities, or frontage work.

Stormwater must be directed and managed without harming neighboring property.

Fire apparatus access, hydrants, water supply, vegetation management, and address visibility may apply.

🧩 The Terms You Must Recognize

Utility availability

Whether service is located or available near the site.

Utility capacity

Whether the system can support the proposed demand.

Connection fee

A charge to connect to a public utility.

Septic reserve area

Backup land preserved for replacement drainfield use.

System-development charge

A fee supporting public infrastructure capacity.

Fire access

Access and water-supply conditions needed for emergency response.

🗺️ Real Parcel Scenario

A parcel is only 300 feet from a water main and 500 feet from power. The seller says utilities are “at the road,” but no provider has reviewed the proposed four-home project.

Open the developer walkthrough
  1. Contact each utility with the exact parcel and proposed use.
  2. Ask about service availability, capacity, extension responsibility, fees, easements, and timing.
  3. Check sewer or septic feasibility separately.
  4. Include road, drainage, and fire-access requirements.
  5. Use written estimates and large contingencies in early analysis.

✅ Think Like a Developer

Your field assignment

  1. Choose one real parcel in your area.
  2. Find its assessor or GIS record.
  3. Write what you know, what you only assume, and what must still be verified.
  4. List the professionals or agencies you would contact next.
  5. Make a preliminary go, investigate, renegotiate, or no-go recommendation.
🧪 Knowledge Check

1. Is a nearby utility line the same as confirmed capacity?

2. Can septic feasibility affect density?

3. Should utility estimates be obtained before purchase?
