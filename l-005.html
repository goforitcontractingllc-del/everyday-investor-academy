Land Due Diligence

Land due diligence is the structured investigation that turns a parcel from a sales story into a verified development decision.

45–70 minutesMultiple micro-lessonsDeveloper thinkingScenario basedAtlas connected
Educational notice: This lesson teaches research and decision-making. It is not a survey, title opinion, engineering report, environmental determination, planning approval, or legal advice.
Why this matters: Most land mistakes come from assumptions that were never confirmed: zoning, access, utilities, flood risk, wetlands, soils, title, slope, or approval timing.
Micro-lessonsField scenarioDeveloper assignmentKnowledge check

5.1 — Legal and Planning Review

Confirm ownership, title exceptions, legal access, zoning, overlays, comprehensive-plan designation, and existing approvals.

Read the actual code sections instead of relying only on a zoning label.

Ask planning staff specific written questions about the proposed use.

5.2 — Physical Site Review

Study topography, drainage, soils, vegetation, rock, wildfire exposure, flood zones, wetlands, and contamination concerns.

Walk the site in different conditions when practical.

Use qualified professionals when a constraint could change buildability.

5.3 — Financial Review

Add acquisition, closing, studies, entitlement, engineering, utility, road, permit, financing, tax, insurance, and holding costs.

Use expected, conservative, and failure scenarios.

Create a maximum acceptable land price based on the completed project—not the seller’s asking price.

5.4 — Approval and Exit Review

Identify every decision-maker, document, hearing, review, and possible appeal.

Estimate how long money may be tied up before construction or resale.

Write an exit plan if approvals fail, costs rise, or the market changes.

🧩 The Terms You Must Recognize

Zoning

Local rules controlling allowed uses and development standards.

Overlay

An added regulatory layer affecting a mapped area.

Flood zone

A mapped flood-risk category requiring further review.

Wetland

Land with hydrology, soils, and vegetation that may trigger protections.

Feasibility period

Contract time allowed for investigation before closing.

Go/no-go decision

The documented choice to proceed, renegotiate, or stop.

🗺️ Real Parcel Scenario

A one-acre parcel is zoned for housing and priced below nearby lots. Early review shows steep rear slope, possible mapped floodplain, no sewer connection, and a utility easement through the center.

Open the developer walkthrough
  1. Map every constraint rather than evaluating them separately.
  2. Ask whether the remaining buildable area can support the intended use and setbacks.
  3. Price engineering, drainage, septic, access, and utility relocation risks.
  4. Use a feasibility period with clear termination rights.
  5. Walk away when the verified project no longer meets the return or housing goal.

✅ Think Like a Developer

Your field assignment

  1. Choose one real parcel in your area.
  2. Find its assessor or GIS record.
  3. Write what you know, what you only assume, and what must still be verified.
  4. List the professionals or agencies you would contact next.
  5. Make a preliminary go, investigate, renegotiate, or no-go recommendation.
🧪 Knowledge Check

1. Should zoning labels be checked against actual code?

2. Can several small constraints combine into a major problem?

3. Should a maximum land price be based on the completed project?
