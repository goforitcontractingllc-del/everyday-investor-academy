Subdivisions, Partitions, and Creating Lots

Dividing land creates new legal parcels, but the process also creates roads, utilities, drainage, public improvements, surveys, dedications, and long-term maintenance responsibilities.

Why this matters: The number of theoretical lots is rarely the number of finished lots. Infrastructure and approval conditions consume land, time, and capital.

8.1 — Partition vs. Subdivision

A partition generally creates a small number of parcels, while a subdivision creates more lots under state and local rules.

Definitions and thresholds must be verified in the current jurisdiction.

Both processes require formal review and recorded documents.

8.2 — Preliminary Approval

Preliminary plans show lot layout, roads, utilities, drainage, open space, and compliance.

Conditions of approval may require redesign or expensive public improvements.

Approval expiration dates and extension rules matter.

8.3 — Final Plat and Recording

A surveyor prepares the final plat after conditions are satisfied.

Signatures, taxes, bonds, dedications, easements, and improvement assurances may be required.

New lots do not legally exist merely because a sketch was approved.

8.4 — Infrastructure and Public Improvements

Developers may need to build or improve streets, sidewalks, water, sewer, stormwater, lighting, and utilities.

Frontage improvements can apply even when work occurs outside the parcel.

Maintenance may shift to a city, county, HOA, utility, or private owners depending on acceptance and agreements.

🧩 The Terms You Must Recognize

Partition

A formal land division creating a limited number of parcels.

Subdivision

A formal land division creating multiple lots.

Preliminary plat

The proposed layout reviewed before final recording.

Final plat

The survey document recorded to legally create lots.

Dedication

Transfer or reservation of land for public use.

Performance bond

Financial security guaranteeing required improvements.

🗺️ Real Parcel Scenario

A developer sketches eight lots on a three-acre parcel. After adding a public street, stormwater facility, utility easements, and required setbacks, only five lots remain practical.

Open the developer walkthrough
  1. Start with code-compliant street and utility geometry.
  2. Reserve land for drainage and public improvements.
  3. Test lot dimensions, access, setbacks, and utility service.
  4. Estimate all off-site and frontage work.
  5. Calculate profit using the realistic finished-lot count.
