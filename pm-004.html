PM-004 · Property Management

Owners, Residents, and Vendors

Understand the different goals, authority, expectations, and boundaries among the people connected to a rental property.

20–30 minute readEvergreen coreReviewed July 2026

Start here

Most property-management conflict is not caused by a building. It comes from unclear roles, mismatched expectations, incomplete information, or someone making a promise they were not authorized to make.

Three groups see the same problem differently

Owner

Sees cost, risk, long-term value, insurance, cash flow, authority, and whether the management agreement is being followed.

Resident

Sees safety, privacy, comfort, response time, disruption, fairness, and whether the home works as expected.

Vendor

Sees scope, access, hazards, materials, schedule, approval, completion standard, and payment.

Manager

Must connect all three perspectives while following the lease, management agreement, policy, law, and available facts.

Authority prevents accidental promises

A resident may ask for an improvement. A vendor may recommend replacement. An owner may want the lowest-cost repair. The manager should not promise the resident a result before confirming authority and should not tell the vendor to proceed beyond an approved scope.

A clear response sounds like: “I understand what you are requesting. I need to confirm the condition, review the agreement, and obtain the required approval before I can promise the next step.”

Boundaries are not bad service

  • Residents should know how and where to submit requests.
  • Owners should know when the manager needs a decision.
  • Vendors should know who may authorize changes.
  • Managers should not become the personal messenger for unrelated disputes.
  • After-hours channels should be reserved for defined urgent situations.
  • Abusive communication can be stopped and redirected into a documented process.

Example: the fence request

A resident wants a new fence for privacy. The owner is concerned about cost. The contractor says several posts are failing and recommends full replacement. The manager’s role is to gather condition evidence, clarify whether there is a safety or lease issue, obtain written options and costs, explain resident impact, and ask the owner for a decision.

The manager does not have to choose a side. The manager has to create a decision that is informed, authorized, documented, and communicated.

Communication should answer five things

  1. What happened?
  2. What is known and still unknown?
  3. What is the risk or deadline?
  4. What decision or action is needed?
  5. When will the next update occur?

Common mistakes

  • Making verbal promises that are not written into the lease or approval.
  • Sending vendors vague descriptions such as “fix the bathroom.”
  • Allowing residents to negotiate work directly with vendors without controls.
  • Forwarding emotional messages without summarizing the actual decision needed.
  • Hiding bad news from the owner.
  • Using residents to communicate with the owner or vice versa.

Build a responsibility map

Create four columns: owner, resident, vendor, and manager. For rent, repairs, emergencies, entry, landscaping, utilities, and approvals, write who reports, who decides, who performs, who pays, and who records the result.
