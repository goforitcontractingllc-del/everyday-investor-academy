The Management Agreement

Learn how the agreement defines authority, scope, fees, money, communication, owner duties, and the end of the relationship.

20–30 minute readEvergreen coreReviewed July 2026

Start here

The management agreement is the operating constitution between an owner and a property manager. It explains what the manager is hired to do, what they may approve, how they are paid, and what the owner must provide.

Why the agreement matters

Without a clear agreement, routine questions become conflict: Can the manager approve an emergency repair? Who holds the security deposit? Is lease renewal included? What happens if the owner refuses a required repair? Who pays legal or inspection costs? How can either side terminate?

The agreement should match the services actually offered and the authority actually needed to perform them.

Core sections to understand

  • Parties and property: correct ownership, manager, business name, and covered property.
  • Term and termination: start date, renewal, notice, final accounting, records, keys, and transition.
  • Scope: marketing, screening, leasing, rent collection, maintenance, inspections, accounting, notices, and other services.
  • Authority: spending limit, emergency action, vendor selection, lease signing, notices, and legal referral.
  • Fees: monthly management, leasing, renewal, inspection, project coordination, setup, cancellation, and other charges.
  • Owner duties: reserves, insurance, safe property condition, truthful information, lawful instructions, and timely decisions.
  • Money: trust accounts, deposits, reserves, bills, distributions, shortages, and final accounting.
  • Communication: reporting, approvals, emergency contacts, and response expectations.

Authority and spending limits

A common structure allows routine work up to a stated amount, while larger work needs owner approval. Emergencies often require broader authority to prevent injury or additional property damage.

The amount alone is not enough. The agreement should explain what counts as an emergency, how attempts to reach the owner are handled, and whether the manager can act when waiting would create greater harm.

Fees should connect to actual work

A monthly percentage may cover ordinary management, but not every task. Leasing, major projects, court appearances, insurance claims, extensive renovation, after-hours response, or unusual travel may have separate pricing.

Example: At $1,700 monthly rent, a 9% management fee equals $153. That fee does not automatically include every possible service. The agreement should make the boundaries visible.

Owner instructions cannot override law

A manager should not follow an instruction that would require discrimination, unsafe conditions, mishandling money, improper entry, or another unlawful practice. A professional agreement should allow the manager to refuse unlawful instructions and, when necessary, terminate the relationship.

Taking over an occupied property

The agreement should address existing leases, deposits, ledgers, notices, resident disputes, pending work, warranties, keys, and records. The manager needs to know what they are accepting and what remains the owner’s responsibility.

Common mistakes

  • Using a generic agreement that does not match the business.
  • Leaving emergency authority vague.
  • Failing to disclose additional fees.
  • Accepting properties without required reserves.
  • Failing to define who holds deposits and other trust funds.
  • Not explaining what happens to records and money at termination.
  • Allowing owners to communicate directly with residents in ways that undermine management.

Agreement review exercise

Take a sample management agreement and highlight five categories in different colors: services, authority, money, owner duties, and termination. Any paragraph that does not fit one of those categories should be examined to understand why it exists.

Oregon professional-practice note

Current through July 2026: Oregon law regulates professional rental-property management, including license activity and property-management agreements. A licensed property manager may engage in management of rental real estate under a property-management agreement with the owner. Current licensing and business requirements should be checked directly with the Oregon Real Estate Agency and ORS Chapter 696 before services are offered.
