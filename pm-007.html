Tenant Screening

Build written criteria, verify information, make consistent decisions, and protect fair-housing compliance.

20–30 minute readEvergreen coreReviewed July 2026

Start here

Tenant screening is a documented comparison between verified information and prewritten criteria. It is not a personality contest or a search for a “perfect tenant.”

Start with written criteria

Criteria should exist before applications arrive. They should explain what will be considered, how information will be verified, and what may lead to approval, conditional approval, or denial.

Quick take: The manager should be able to explain the decision by pointing to the same written process offered to every applicant.

A basic workflow

  1. Publish criteria and process.
  2. Record when a complete application arrives.
  3. Collect only necessary information.
  4. Verify identity and permission for reports.
  5. Verify income and lawful qualifications.
  6. Contact rental references with standard questions.
  7. Review permitted credit, eviction, or criminal information carefully.
  8. Compare to criteria.
  9. Issue the decision and required notices.
  10. Securely retain or dispose of records.

Income comes in different forms

Applicants may have wages, self-employment income, benefits, retirement, child support, housing assistance, savings, or other lawful sources. A verification policy should explain acceptable documentation without collecting unnecessary private details.

Ask factual rental-history questions

  • Dates of tenancy.
  • Rent amount and documented payment history.
  • Documented lease violations.
  • Condition at move-out.
  • Balance owed and whether disputed.
  • Whether the reference is the actual owner or authorized manager.

Avoid vague questions such as whether someone was “a good tenant.”

Reports need context

Credit, criminal, and eviction records can be wrong, incomplete, old, or unrelated to current tenancy ability. Blanket exclusions may also raise fair-housing concerns. Managers need current legal review, relevance standards, and a process for required notices or applicant responses.

Accommodation and access

An applicant may request a communication change, additional time, assistance completing a step, or another disability-related accommodation. Route that request through the accommodation process and protect confidentiality.

Data security

  • Limit staff access.
  • Use secure portals and passwords.
  • Do not leave printed applications visible.
  • Collect only necessary data.
  • Follow retention and destruction policies.

Common mistakes

  • Changing criteria after seeing an applicant.
  • Using “gut feeling.”
  • Giving different applicants different chances.
  • Failing to verify references.
  • Overbroad criminal-history exclusions.
  • Discussing protected or private information.
  • Weak data security.

Practice exercise

Draw six boxes: application received, completeness review, verification, criteria comparison, decision, and notice. Under each, list the record proving the step occurred.

Check your understanding

  1. Why must criteria exist first?
  2. Why are factual references stronger?
  3. How can accommodation affect the process?
  4. Why is data security part of screening?
Open the answer guide

Prewritten criteria reduce arbitrary decisions; factual questions create comparable evidence; an accommodation may change how a step is completed; secure data handling protects applicants and the integrity of the process.

Oregon update note

Current through July 2026: ORS Chapter 90 defines applicant screening charges and contains Oregon-specific application provisions. Oregon’s April 2026 legislative guide states that a screening charge may cover only the landlord’s actual average screening cost. Verify current statutes, local rules, and notices before screening.
